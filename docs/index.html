Listening examples

Each example has three versions of the same clip: the dry stems, the processed version, and the dry stems rendered through the settings FX-JEPA predicted from the first two.

Credits

Each row plays one version, and switching rows within an example keeps the playback position, which makes it possible to flip between the processed version and our render at the same moment in the clip. We level-matched the three versions of each clip before encoding, because in a quick A/B the louder version tends to sound better, and without matching a level difference could pass for a difference in processing.

Each example is one of the 1,000 held-out clips and comes from a Free Music Archive (FMA) song. The processed version was made with our own effect strip, built from open-source effects, using a known chain, so these examples are in-domain, and the render uses the chain predicted by our best model (conv first layer + slot readout). Because the dry stems are separated from a finished FMA release, they already carry the artist's own mix, and "dry" here means only that they come before our chain. Stems that were silent in the excerpt are left out of all three versions, as they are in scoring. These clips illustrate the task and are not a listening test, and a handful of examples cannot show average quality. The results page has the measured numbers: across the 1,000 held-out clips, handing back the dry audio scores 1.17 MRSTFT and the best model averages 0.413, where MRSTFT is a spectral distance between our render and the reference and lower is better.

We chose the 8 examples from held-out clips of FMA tracks whose Creative Commons license allows derivatives, picking them so that all 11 effect types appear somewhere in the set. Their MRSTFT runs from 0.156 to 0.550 against the mean of 0.413 over all 1,000 held-out clips, so 5 of the 8 are better than average and the last three (0.422, 0.446 and 0.550) are worse.

Every song comes from the Free Music Archive under a Creative Commons license that allows derivatives. We excerpted 10 seconds of each song, split it into four stems, processed each stem through an effects chain (the true one or the predicted one), summed the stems again, level-matched the three versions and encoded them as MP3. Nothing here comes from Universal Audio products or data.
