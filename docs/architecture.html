Architecture

How FX-JEPA reads the audio

Two recordings go in: dry stems and a processed version of the same stems. One grid comes out: for each stem, which effects are on and where their knobs are set. This page walks through every piece in plain language.

The task

A stem is one instrument group's own track. We use four: vocals, bass, drums and other. The dry stems are the four stems before our chain. They are separated from a finished Free Music Archive release, or are MoisesDB studio stems, so they may already carry the artist's own processing; dry means before our strip, not untouched. The the processed version is the same stems after someone ran them through a chain of effects. A chain here is a fixed channel strip of 11 effects, each of which can be switched on or off, with 28 knobs between them. FX-JEPA predicts, for each of the 4 stems, which of the 11 effects are on and the settings of the 28 knobs. That's 44 switches and 112 knobs, the whole action space.

The strip is built from open-source effects, and it's the same one used to make the training data. Each training example draws fresh on/off switches and knob values. A different effects library, Pedalboard, is kept for testing only, so that test is out of domain for every model.

The walkthrough

1

Two recordings in, as pictures of sound

Each example is two 10-second recordings of the same 4 stems: the dry stems and their processed version. Each recording becomes a log-mel spectrogram, a picture of sound with time running across, frequency running up and brightness showing energy. Mel means the frequency axis is spaced the way hearing is, finer at low frequencies. Log means energy is on a decibel-like scale. Four stems in stereo give 8 channels, each 80 mel bands by 862 frames of 11.6 ms. Phase is dropped. The same network, with the same weights, encodes the dry and the processed recording.

Each spectrogram is then rescaled per channel (InstanceNorm), which removes absolute level, the balance between stems and the level difference between left and right. Gain, pan and width live in exactly those numbers, so step 2 hands them back.

2

Hand-measured features, added through FiLM

Before training, 64 numbers are measured from each recording with ordinary signal-processing code: loudness, spectral balance and stereo measurements. They're StemFX's 64 mixing features, which StemFX adapted from Diff-MST. They enter the network through FiLM (feature-wise linear modulation): a small network turns the 64 numbers into one scale and one shift per channel, applied inside every transformer block. A further 68 stereo measurements join the readout in step 5.

We tested nine variants of these features. None did better than keeping them as they are, and removing them hurt stereo width. They aren't essential to the overall score, though: the FX-Encoder++ design trained with our recipe has no hand features and still lands level with our baseline at 70k steps (one run, within run-to-run noise).

3

The first layer: from flat patches to a small conv stem

Our baseline is a vision transformer (ViT). A ViT cuts the spectrogram into 16 by 16 patches and turns each patch into one token, a list of 192 numbers, with a single linear map. At 11.6 ms per frame a patch spans 186 ms. The grid is 5 rows of mel bands by 53 columns of time, so 265 tokens, plus one summary token.

A linear map can't mark "an onset happened here" before it averages over the patch, so a 120 ms echo inside a 186 ms patch looks like a slightly longer note. A matched CNN with a nonlinear first layer read delay time much better at the same overall score, which pointed at the first layer.

The conv first layer (a small "conv stem") replaces the linear patch map with a 7 by 7 convolution at the full frame rate, then three strided 3 by 3 convolutions, then a linear projection, landing on the same 5 by 53 grid. The idea comes from Xiao et al., "Early Convolutions Help Transformers See Better". On its own it lowered delay-time error, improved reverb room size, and left the overall score where it was (inside run-to-run noise). It improves delay and reverb timing; it doesn't fix them. GPU speed stays within 1% of our baseline.

4

The trunk: ViT-Tiny, 12 blocks

Twelve transformer blocks, width 192, 3 attention heads, over the 266 tokens. Attention lets each token look at every other token and take a weighted average of them, with the weights computed from the content. "Tiny" is the size name: about 7.1M parameters in the encoder. Earlier we tested ViT-Tiny, ViT-Small and a matched CNN, and they tied in-domain, so the trunk isn't where the score is decided.

5

The readout: one summary, or 44 named slots

Our baseline's readout keeps only the summary token of each recording. The dry summary, the processed summary and their difference are fused into one 512-number vector, the 68 stereo measurements are added, and a residual MLP turns that vector into 44 switch scores and 112 knob values. Every answer about every stem and every effect has to squeeze through those 512 numbers.

The slot readout asks 44 named questions instead, one per stem and effect. It learns 4 stem vectors and 11 effect vectors and adds them, so "vocals" plus "delay" is the vocals-delay query. That's 44 queries from 15 learned vectors, so what a delay looks like is shared across stems. Each query reads all of the dry and processed tokens through cross-attention, and can also see the fused vector our baseline uses. There are four blocks, and in each one the 44 slots also attend to each other, which lets the four delay slots work out which stem has the echo. Each slot then predicts its own switch and its own knobs.

The slot readout has fewer parameters than the MLP it replaces (about 2.5M against 7.0M), so model size doesn't explain the improvement. On its own, on top of our baseline, it cut MRSTFT by 10.9%. That's a pre-registered formal call, and the readout is the only thing that differs between the two models. Reading stems out with slot queries follows Stembed; ours are named in advance, so there's no matching step and no contrastive loss.

6

Training: the JEPA objective plus a knob loss

JEPA stands for joint-embedding predictive architecture. "Joint embedding" means audio and effect settings are mapped into one shared space of vectors; "predictive" means one side is trained to predict the other. Here a second small network, the settings encoder, turns the true chain into a vector, and the audio side's embedding of the dry and processed pair is trained to predict that vector. We follow the view of Synth-JEPA, where an audio encoder and a synthesizer-parameter encoder predict each other. With effects the sound also depends on the source, so our audio side encodes a dry and processed pair. SIGReg, a regulariser from LeJEPA, keeps the embeddings spread out instead of collapsing to one point.

The knob loss is plain regression on the 44 switches (binary cross-entropy) and 112 knob values (squared error), and it's the main teacher. The whole loss, as trained, is

L = (1 - 2λ) / 3 · (Linv + Lfwd + Lsrc) + λ (Sa + Sp) + Lreg + 0.5 (Ladv + LD + Lst), with λ = 0.02.

Linv: the audio embedding predicts the settings embedding, as a squared error against a stop-gradient target. Lfwd: the settings embedding predicts the audio embedding. Lsrc: the settings embedding plus the embedding of the dry stems alone predict each song's pair embedding. Sa, Sp: SIGReg on the audio and settings branches, which keeps each set of embeddings spread out. Lreg: the knob loss. Ladv, LD, Lst: three terms on the settings side, a margin that keeps the true chain closer than an attacked or resampled one, and two heads that predict hand-measured features and stereo balance from the settings embedding; none of them reaches the audio encoder. The code also carries a dispersion term for several songs sharing one chain; every training step here had one pair per chain, so it was zero throughout and is left out. This total reproduces the logged training loss of the best model's second run. Of the terms that reach the audio encoder, the knob loss has weight 1.0, each JEPA prediction term 0.32 and SIGReg 0.02 on each side. The JEPA side trains alongside the rest and isn't used at prediction time.

The caveat. On an out-of-domain held-out test scored once (Pedalboard effects on MUSDB18 test songs), JEPA-trained heads tied a control: the same encoder trained on the knob loss alone. We haven't shown that the JEPA objective improves MRSTFT over plain regression. The improvements on the poster come from the first layer and the readout. Isolating what the predictive objective adds is future work.

7

Render once, then score

At test time the model sees the dry and processed stems once and writes out a chain. The dry stems are rendered once through that chain, and the render is compared with the processed version. That comparison is MRSTFT, the multi-resolution STFT distance between our render and the reference, over all 8 stem channels (4 stems, left and right). Lower is better, and doing nothing scores 1.17. No candidate is rendered before the choice. How MRSTFT is computed.

What "frozen + our readout" means

We take the released checkpoint, freeze it so its weights don't change, and train a small readout on top of its embeddings to predict the same 44 switches and 112 knobs. Each readout gets 50,000 training examples and up to 40 epochs, keeping the epoch with the best held-out loss, and is scored on the same 1,000 held-out clips with the same code as our models. This tests the released representation on our task. It doesn't test the design, and it's a smaller training budget than our models get, so a stronger readout could narrow the gap. The readouts also didn't get a label fix that our best model had. In their favour, 761 of the 1,000 queries were used to pick each readout's best epoch; on the 239 queries that weren't, the picture is the same (StemFX 0.867 against our baseline 0.494).

Why we also train the designs our way

To separate the design from the released weights, we trained two of the published designs from random weights with our baseline's data, loss and budget. StemFX's BSFiLM design, trained our way, scores 0.485, within noise of our baseline (0.492). FX-Encoder++'s published encoder (per-stem encoding, no hand features) scores 0.463 at 70k of its 108k training steps: level with our baseline at 70k (within run-to-run noise), one run. Its own run-to-run spread isn't measured, so there's no formal call (the learning curve). The gap between 0.897 frozen and 0.463 trained our way says the frozen number measures how well the released weights fit our task, not what the design can do. Most of the distance between the published encoders and our models comes from training. The encoder design moves the score less than the readout does.

Questions people ask

Answers to the questions readers asked about the poster. Every number is on the 1,000 held-out clips unless the answer names another test.

The task and the claim

Is this a representation paper or a parameter-estimation paper?

It estimates effect settings from a dry and processed pair. There is some evidence about the representation too. In an earlier comparison where every encoder was frozen and got the same small readout, our embedding scored 0.636 MRSTFT against 0.842 to 0.871 for StemFX, AFx-Rep and FX-Encoder++ (200 held-out pairs, in-domain, which favours ours), and 1.14 against 1.21 to 1.84 on the out-of-domain Pedalboard test, a held-out test scored once. The slot readout didn't change the pooled embedding: its retrieval top-1 is 0.958 with the conv first layer alone and with the slot readout added.

If the JEPA objective ties a knob-loss-only control, why call it FX-JEPA? Isn't this regression with extra losses?

Mostly, yes, and we say so. The audio encoder's gradient gets weight 1.0 from the knob loss, 0.32 from the JEPA prediction term and 0.02 from SIGReg. On the Pedalboard held-out test scored once, the JEPA-trained model tied the same encoder trained on the knob loss alone (difference +0.014 MRSTFT, interval -0.026 to +0.052). FX-JEPA names the recipe and the model family. The JEPA side also yields settings and pair embeddings for later use; it hasn't been shown to improve MRSTFT.

What if the reference is a different song, or was made with effects outside your 11?

Right now this is chain inversion: the reference is the same stems run through our strip. Cross-song was tested only on earlier models, on the Pedalboard set with a chain applied to one song and the answer scored on another: the model scored 1.044, the knob-loss-only control 1.042 and doing nothing 1.703. Of the 14 Pedalboard test effects, our strip makes 8 exactly, the shelves and the compressor only approximately, and chorus and phaser not at all. A reference from outside the strip always gets some chain back, and nothing flags it.

Where do I get processed stems? I usually have a stereo reference mix.

Today you'd need them: the input is the 4 processed stems of the same performance. Mix-only references, or splitting a reference mix into stems first, haven't been tested on these models.

Does it choose the effects and their order, or set the knobs of a fixed chain?

It sets a fixed chain: 11 effects in a fixed order (HPF, low shelf, peak EQ, high shelf, LPF, compressor, saturation, clipper, delay, reverb, output), each switched on or off, with its knobs. The order isn't predicted.

"Audition more options faster": faster than what?

We have no user timing. The model costs one forward pass: 0.29 s per clip for the best model, warm, one clip at a time, on one GPU (0.27 to 0.28 s for the baseline). The search comparison below was timed on the 5 October harness with earlier models, where the readout alone took about 0.1 s per example. A render-and-compare search on the same harness (CMA-ES, 512 renders) took about 7.4 s per target and scored worse than the model. The model returns one chain per stem, rendered once, not a ranked list.

Is the settings grid on the poster a real prediction?

No. The grid, the example settings under it and the render are illustrations. The listen page has real predictions, with the true and predicted chains for each example.

The model

Is the effect chain differentiable during training?

There's no render in training at all, differentiable or otherwise. The losses are on settings and embeddings. Rendering happens once, at test time.

How is the slot readout different from DETR-style queries?

It's the same mechanism, learned queries with cross-attention, but the queries are named: a stem vector plus an effect vector gives 44 queries from 15 learned vectors, so no matching step is needed. It has four blocks of slot self-attention, cross-attention over the 532 dry and processed tokens, and an MLP, about 2.5M parameters in place of a 7.0M MLP. The idea of reading stems out with slot queries comes from Stembed. What we add is the measurement: on our baseline this readout cut MRSTFT by 10.9%, while the JEPA objective tied a knob-loss-only control on a held-out test scored once, with earlier models.

The true settings go into the settings encoder. Isn't the whole thing supervised?

Yes. The settings encoder sees the true settings, and the knob loss is a regression. The JEPA terms are a joint-embedding objective between audio and settings, not self-supervised pretraining on unlabelled audio. We didn't run an unlabelled pretrain-then-probe setup.

What does the settings encoder buy at prediction time?

Nothing directly: the settings encoder and the predictors are dropped, and the readout predicts on its own. The ablation is the knob-loss-only control, the same encoder without the JEPA terms. It tied on the Pedalboard held-out test scored once (+0.014, interval -0.026 to +0.052) and on the cross-song version (+0.002, interval -0.072 to +0.067). That comparison hasn't been repeated for the best model.

Why doesn't the embedding collapse? Is SIGReg on both sides?

SIGReg, from LeJEPA, runs on both the audio and the settings embedding at weight 0.02 each; the poster figure draws it on one branch only. An early screen varied SIGReg strength on an earlier setup. There's no run without SIGReg and no VICReg comparison on the current models.

Why a Perceiver for the settings?

It follows Synth-JEPA's settings encoder (32 latents, 8 blocks), one token per true knob. We didn't ablate it.

What are the 64 hand features, and do the baselines get them?

They're StemFX's 64 mixing features (loudness, spectral balance and stereo measurements), adapted from Diff-MST, fed in through FiLM, plus 68 stereo measurements in the readout. The StemFX design trained our way has them. The FX-Encoder++ design trained our way has none and still scores 0.463 at 70k steps. Removing them from our baseline leaves MRSTFT within noise but drops stereo-width skill from 0.21 to 0.02. Giving AFx-Rep's frozen readout measured stem levels changed nothing (0.894).

Why a ViT with a conv first layer, when a CNN scores close?

In earlier tests ViT-Tiny, ViT-Small and a matched CNN tied in-domain, so the trunk doesn't decide the score. The conv first layer was added because a linear 186 ms patch can't mark an onset; the idea is from Xiao et al. A CNN with the slot readout wasn't run.

Why log-mel? It drops phase and the transient detail attack and release need.

The input is 80 mel bands, FFT 2048, hop 512 at 44.1 kHz (11.6 ms frames), with phase discarded. Compressor attack and release stay near guessing for every model. An earlier study that swapped in the true value one knob group at a time put them at about 0.1% of MRSTFT, so they don't move the headline.

Hard or soft switches? Many chains make the same sound. How do you handle that?

Switches are trained as on/off probabilities and decoded at 0.5, with at least the most likely effect switched on per stem. Knobs are regressed directly on values mapped to 0 to 1, so equivalent settings get averaged; nothing models the ambiguity. Training labels mark an inaudible effect as off, for example a compressor with under 1 dB of gain reduction or an EQ band under 0.5 dB. MRSTFT scores the sound and knob skill scores the parameters, which is why both are shown.

What's the knob loss when an effect is off?

It's masked: the knob error counts only the knobs of effects that are on in the true chain. Knob skill is also scored only where the effect is on.

The numbers

Is our baseline's 0.492 the better of two runs?

No. 0.492 is the first run and the reference model; the second run (0.504) was trained later to measure noise. The same goes for the best model: 0.413 is the first run, 0.443 the second.

Why a second run for the best model but not for baseline + slot readout?

Training budget. The best model's second run was trained as the noise ruler. Baseline + slot readout has one run, so its own run-to-run spread isn't measured, though its formal call holds against both runs of our baseline.

Are the knob improvements from the conv layer, the hand features or the label fix?

Not the hand features: every model in our baseline's family has them. The label fix removed a false "vocals clip on" label from short chains; its effect on MRSTFT was assumed to be zero and wasn't measured. Baseline + slot readout, which has neither the fix nor the conv layer, separates the parts: its EQ skill matches the best model's, and its median delay miss is 74 ms against the best model's 40 ms.

Frozen published encoders against end-to-end models isn't apples to apples. Isn't the 2x gap just that?

Largely, yes. The same designs trained our way land at 0.485 (StemFX's design) and 0.463 (FX-Encoder++'s design at 70k steps), next to our baseline. The frozen rows test released weights through a readout trained on 50,000 examples without the label fix, and 761 of the 1,000 queries were used to pick each readout's best epoch, which favours them; the other 239 queries give the same picture. An earlier frozen-against-frozen check, with the same readout for every encoder, still favoured ours: 0.636 against 0.842 to 0.871.

How does the frozen readouts' budget compare with your training?

Each frozen readout gets 50,000 training examples and up to 40 epochs. Our models train for 108,000 updates at batch 256, about 27.6M pair presentations.

The FX-Encoder++ design trained your way scores below your baseline. Is that settled?

No. It's a snapshot at 70k of 108k steps from one run: 0.463 against our baseline's 0.492, a 5.9% gap, with a paired interval of -0.046 to -0.013 MRSTFT that doesn't cross zero. That gap sits inside the baseline family's run-to-run spread of about 6%, and this design's own spread isn't measured, so there's no formal call. Level with our baseline at 70k, one run; the 108k score goes in the paper.

Is there a ceiling? What does rendering the true settings score?

Re-rendering the true chain reproduces the reference, so the floor is about 0 (0.003 or exactly 0 in our checks). MRSTFT between two different chains that sound alike hasn't been measured.

Are the bars means or medians?

Means over the 1,000 queries. Per-query scores exist, but we haven't plotted their distribution.

Which effects drive the MRSTFT gap?

It hasn't been broken down for the best model. The earlier study on our baseline, which swapped in the true value one knob group at a time, found delay time was its biggest fixable error, with low shelf and clipper near zero and compressor attack and release about 0.1% of MRSTFT.

Is MRSTFT phase-blind? Would a delay that's 40 ms off barely register?

It compares magnitudes only, so it ignores phase. How strongly it penalises a delay-time miss hasn't been measured, which is why delay time gets its own number (how it's measured).

Is a 40 ms delay miss good? Can delay time even be read from the audio?

Delay time runs 50 to 500 ms. 40 ms is the best model's median miss over the 464 stems with a delay in the 1,000 held-out clips, and 33% land within 10%. In our own listening, not a controlled test, that is still a different echo. The lag is in the audio: an autocorrelation of the processed minus the dry signal found it on 107 of 107 stems we checked.

Is switch accuracy reported?

As AUROC per effect for the best model: from 0.884 (low shelf) to 0.999 (LPF), with compressor 0.986, delay 0.982 and reverb 0.998. We don't report F1.

The compressor is near guessing. Is that the data, the representation, or the audio?

The best model's skill is 0.05 for ratio, 0.07 for attack and 0.02 for release; threshold is 0.51. Ratio is scored linearly (1.5 to 10), and rescoring it on a log scale changes almost nothing (0.050 to 0.055). Likely causes: tokens and conv fields average over envelope changes of a few milliseconds, and threshold, ratio and auto makeup gain trade off against each other. We don't have a measurement-based ceiling for the compressor knobs.

Data and effects

How much training data, and are the stems separated or from the studio?

Both. FMA's 20,000-track subset, split into stems with SCNet XL (80,000 stem files), plus MoisesDB studio stems summed to 4 classes. Training uses 10 s windows at 44.1 kHz, about 27.6M pair presentations in all. Hours of audio, distinct pairs and separator artefacts in the dry stems haven't been studied.

How are training chains sampled?

Each effect switches on with a per-stem probability (for vocals: compressor 0.75, reverb 0.6, delay 0.35). Knob values come 70% of the time from a realistic per-stem range and 30% from the full range. Effects too weak to hear are labelled off.

Do training and the held-out clips share songs? Which library renders which test?

The songs are disjoint. The 1,000 held-out clips use the same effect strip as training, so it's in-domain. Pedalboard is used for testing only, so that test is out of domain for every model.

What exactly are the effects?

Filters. HPF and LPF are Butterworth, order 1 or 2 (6 or 12 dB/oct, predicted as a two-way choice). The shelves and the peak are RBJ biquads with shelf Q fixed at 0.707, matching MultiAFx's filters.

Compressor. torchcomp: a one-pole 5 ms RMS detector, stereo-linked, hard knee, auto makeup gain, threshold relative to its input's RMS peak, attack 1 to 50 ms, release 50 to 500 ms.

Saturation and clipper. Saturation is tanh, rescaled to the input's sample peak, with no oversampling. The clipper is a hard clip relative to its input's sample peak. Saturation runs first.

Delay. A feedback comb: 50 to 500 ms, feedback 0 to 0.5, no filter in the loop and no tempo sync.

Reverb. Freeverb, the algorithm behind Pedalboard's reverb, with fixed tunings, so the render is deterministic.

Output. Gain, a balance-style pan, and mid/side width. Every knob's range is on the results page.

Why are saturation and clipper separate? Where are the limiter, de-esser and widener?

The list was chosen as console basics matched to the Pedalboard test, where distortion and clipping are separate effects. A limiter, gate, de-esser, a second peak band, and chorus and phaser were left for later. Stereo width lives in the output stage.
