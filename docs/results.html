Second runs

Run-to-run noise bounds are about 6% for the baseline family and 12% for the best model (98.75% bootstrap bounds over songs; the observed second-run gaps are 2.4% and 7.3%). Spectral only; no listening test.

Two models were trained twice with the same recipe and a different random seed: our baseline (first run 0.492, second run 0.504) and the best model (first run 0.413, second run 0.443). On the chart the bar is the first run, and it's the one every comparison on this page uses; the hollow dot on the FX-JEPA row is the best model's second run. Our baseline's second run is in the table. We didn't keep the better of the two. Every other row is a single run.

The second runs are our ruler for noise. Two runs of our baseline's recipe differ by up to about 6%, and two runs of the best model's recipe by up to about 12% (98.75% bounds from a paired bootstrap over songs). With one pair per recipe these are rulers, not a seed distribution. The observed gaps between the two runs are 2.4% (0.492 to 0.504) and 7.3% (0.413 to 0.443); the bounds are wider than the observed gaps because they come from the bootstrap over songs. A gap smaller than that is treated as noise unless a formal call says otherwise.

A formal call is a pre-registered decision: the endpoint, the interval level and the rule were fixed before the models were trained. The interval comes from a paired bootstrap that resamples songs, not stems, 10,000 times, because stems from one song move together. A call only counts if the new model also comes out ahead of the baseline's second run. Two calls are on the poster: baseline + slot readout against our baseline (-10.9%) and the best model against our baseline (-16.1%, interval -20% to -12%). The StemFX design trained our way has no formal call and sits inside our baseline's noise. The FX-Encoder++ design has no formal call either; see below.

The conv first layer and the slot readout don't show an interaction beyond adding up: the measured interaction is inside the run-to-run floor, so we don't claim one.

The FX-Encoder++ design, trained our way

To separate a published design from its released weights, we trained FX-Encoder++'s encoder from random weights with our baseline's data, loss, batch size and seed. It has no hand features. At 70,000 of its 108,000 training steps it scores 0.463 against our baseline's 0.492, a 5.9% gap. The paired difference per query is -0.029 MRSTFT, with a 95% interval of -0.046 to -0.013 from resampling songs. That interval is reported, not a pre-registered call; the gap is inside the baseline family's run-to-run spread of about 6%, and this design's own spread isn't measured. So the reading is: level with our baseline at 70k, one run. On the 239 queries that no outside readout used for picking an epoch, the gap is smaller: 0.489 against 0.494 (-1%).

The slot readout alone takes our baseline down 10.9%, and that is a formal call.

Knob ranges and how each knob is scored

Each stem has 11 effects with 28 knobs between them. Before the error is measured, every knob is mapped onto 0 to 1 over its range, on the scale in the table; training uses the same mapping. On a log scale a miss counts the same anywhere in the range in ratio terms, so frequency misses read as octaves, and Q, attack and release misses as "times off". Gains and thresholds are already in dB. The two filter slopes are a two-way choice, scored right or wrong. The typical miss is the median error on stems where the true chain has that effect on; the best model's column is its first run.

How delay-time error is measured

Delay time runs from 50 to 500 ms on a linear scale. The error we report is the median absolute miss of the predicted delay-time knob, in milliseconds, over the 464 stems in the 1,000 held-out clips whose true chain has the delay on. Every model is scored on the same 464 stems, and the predicted knob value counts even when the model switched the delay off. Our baseline misses by 93 ms at the median and the best model by 40 ms; 33% of the best model's stems land within 10% of the true time. In our own listening, not a controlled test, a 40 ms miss is still a different echo.

Delay time gets its own number because MRSTFT compares magnitude spectrograms and ignores phase, and how strongly MRSTFT penalises a delay-time miss hasn't been measured.

About the 17% in the abstract

The abstract reports a 17% reduction in spectral error. That was a different test: an out-of-domain held-out set, scored once, processed with Pedalboard effects on MUSDB18 test songs (200 queries from 50 songs), run on earlier models, against StemFX's frozen encoder with our readout. This page shows the 1,000 held-out clips instead: in-domain, on our own effect strip, with the newer models. Both tests give the same ordering, with our trained models ahead of every frozen published encoder under the same readout. Neither is a fresh-song confirmation yet.

The same Pedalboard test is also where the JEPA-trained model tied a control trained on the knob loss alone. That's why we don't credit the improvement to the JEPA objective.

What we don't claim yet

  • The same 1,000 held-out clips were used to select the models; songs outside this set have not been tested.
  • The best model was trained with corrected vocals-clipper labels; our baseline was not.
  • No listening test has been run on these models; the score is spectral only. Statements about what is audible come from our own listening, not a controlled test.
  • No users or engineers have been studied; the workflow around the model is untested.
  • Compressor ratio, attack and release remain near the guessing level.
  • The abstract's 17% was a different test: earlier models, an out-of-domain Pedalboard set, against StemFX's frozen encoder with our readout.
  • Delay feedback and reverb width are also near guessing. In our own listening, not a controlled test, reverb width is close to inaudible on our material.

How MRSTFT is computed

  1. The model sees the dry stems and the processed stems once and predicts a chain: 44 switches and 112 knob values.
  2. The dry stems are rendered once through that chain with the same effect strip.
  3. For each stem, left and right, the render is compared with the processed version using a multi-resolution STFT distance: both are turned into magnitude spectrograms at three window sizes (FFT 512, 1024 and 2048 with hop sizes 128, 256 and 512 and Hann windows the length of the FFT; log magnitudes use an epsilon of 1e-5; short windows catch transients, long ones catch tone), and spectral convergence plus the L1 distance of log magnitudes is averaged over the three. This is the distance StemFX uses.
  4. The 8 channels (4 stems, left and right) are averaged. Stems that are silent in a clip are zeroed so they don't count. That's MRSTFT for one query; the reported number is the mean over the 1,000 queries.

0 means the render is identical to the processed version, and re-rendering the true chain scores close to 0. Handing back the dry audio unchanged scores 1.17. MRSTFT is unitless, computed per stem rather than on a summed mix, and it's a spectral match, not a listening score.

Knob skill, used for the bars, is 1 minus the model's knob error divided by the error of always guessing the typical value, on stems where the effect is on. The scale each knob is scored on is in the knob table.

Data: FMA stems split with SCNet, plus MoisesDB studio stems. Public datasets and open-source effects only. No UA plugins, models or data.

Effects: MultiAFx EQ, torchcomp compressor, tanh saturation, hard clip, delay, Freeverb. Fixed order, 44.1 kHz, 10 s clips. Knob ranges and delay scoring: results page.
