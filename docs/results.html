Second runs

Run-to-run noise bounds are about 6% for the baseline family and 12% for the best model (98.75% bootstrap bounds over songs; the observed second-run gaps are 2.4% and 7.3%). Spectral only; no listening test.

A single training run cannot separate a difference between two models from the luck of the random seed, so we trained two models twice with the same recipe and a different seed, our baseline (first run 0.492, second run 0.504) and the best model (first run 0.413, second run 0.443). The first run is the one every comparison on this page uses, and both second runs are in the table above. We did not keep the better of the two runs, and every other row is a single run.

We use the second runs as a ruler for noise. From a paired bootstrap over songs (98.75% bounds), two runs of our baseline's recipe may differ by up to about 6%, and two runs of the best model's recipe by up to about 12%. The observed gaps between the two runs are smaller, 2.4% (0.492 to 0.504) and 7.3% (0.413 to 0.443), because the bounds also carry the uncertainty from which songs happen to be in the test set. With one pair of runs per recipe, these bounds give a rough scale for seed noise and say little about its distribution, and we treat any gap smaller than the bound as noise unless a formal call says otherwise.

A formal call is a pre-registered decision, in which the endpoint, the interval level and the decision rule were fixed before the models were trained, so that the test could not be chosen after seeing which one favoured the new model. The interval comes from a paired bootstrap that resamples songs rather than stems, 10,000 times, because stems from one song move together, and resampling them one by one would make the interval narrower than the evidence supports. A call counts only if the new model also comes out ahead of our baseline's second run. The poster carries two calls, baseline + slot readout against our baseline (-10.9%) and the best model against our baseline (-16.1%, interval -20% to -12%). The StemFX design trained from scratch on our data has no formal call and sits inside our baseline's noise, and the FX-Encoder++ design has no formal call either (see below).

We do not claim an interaction between the conv first layer and the slot readout (i.e., a combined gain larger than the two gains added together), because the measured interaction falls inside the run-to-run floor.

The FX-Encoder++ design, trained our way

A frozen checkpoint tests the released weights on our task and says little about the design behind them, so to separate the two we trained FX-Encoder++'s encoder from scratch (i.e., from random weights) with our baseline's data, loss, batch size and seed; this design has no hand features. At 70,000 of its 108,000 training steps it scores 0.463 against our baseline's 0.492, a 5.9% gap, and the paired difference per query is -0.029 MRSTFT with a 95% interval of -0.046 to -0.013 from resampling songs. We report that interval without treating it as a pre-registered call, because the gap is inside the baseline family's run-to-run spread of about 6% and this design's own spread has not been measured. We therefore read it as level with our baseline (within run-to-run noise), one run at 70k of 108k steps. On the 239 queries that no outside readout used for picking an epoch, the gap is smaller, 0.489 against 0.494 (-1%).

For comparison, changing only the readout of our baseline lowers MRSTFT by 10.9%, a formal call, so in our runs the readout moves the score more than this change of encoder design does.

The knobs are measured in units as different as octaves, decibels and milliseconds, so knob skill puts them on one scale by comparing the model's error with the error of always guessing the typical value, where 0 means no better than that guess and 1 means perfect; without this, a knob whose true values cluster tightly would look well predicted even if the model had learned nothing about it. Skill is scored only on stems where that effect is on, and 0.47 means about half the error of guessing (it is not a 47% hit rate). The median misses are on the same stems, which for delay time is 464 stems (how delay-time error is measured).

Because this comparison sets our baseline against the best model, it mixes both changes, and baseline + slot readout, which has no conv first layer, is the model that separates them. The EQ improvement comes from the readout, since the slot readout alone reaches 0.78 skill on peak frequency and 0.71 on peak gain, and so does most of the reverb improvement (room size goes from 0.18 to 0.30 with the readout alone and to 0.39 with both changes, damping from 0.03 to 0.14 and 0.18). Delay time improves mostly with the conv first layer, with a median miss of 93 ms for our baseline, 74 ms with the readout alone and 40 ms with both, which is consistent with the reason the layer was added (a linear patch cannot mark where an echo starts).

Knob ranges and how each knob is scored

Each stem has 11 effects with 28 knobs between them. Before the error is measured, every knob is mapped onto 0 to 1 over its range on the scale given in the table, and training uses the same mapping. Frequencies, Q, attack and release use a log scale because these knobs are heard in ratios (e.g., a 100 Hz miss is a full octave at 100 Hz and a small fraction of one at 8 kHz), so on that scale a miss counts the same anywhere in the range and reads as octaves for frequency or as "times off" for Q, attack and release. Gains and thresholds are already in dB, and the two filter slopes are a two-way choice scored as right or wrong. The typical miss is the median error on stems where the true chain has that effect on, and the best model's column is its first run.

How delay-time error is measured

Delay time runs from 50 to 500 ms on a linear scale. The error we report is the median absolute miss of the predicted delay-time knob, in milliseconds, over the 464 stems in the 1,000 held-out clips whose true chain has the delay on. Every model is scored on the same 464 stems, and the predicted knob value counts even when the model switched the delay off. Our baseline misses by 93 ms at the median and the best model by 40 ms; 33% of the best model's stems land within 10% of the true time. In our own listening, not a controlled test, a 40 ms miss is still a different echo.

We report delay time as its own number because MRSTFT compares magnitude spectrograms and ignores phase, and we have not measured how strongly it penalises a delay-time miss, so a small change in MRSTFT could hide an audible difference in echo timing.

What we do not claim

  • The same 1,000 held-out clips were used to select the models; songs outside this set have not been tested.
  • The best model was trained with corrected vocals-clipper labels; our baseline was not.
  • No listening test has been run on these models; the score is spectral only. Statements about what is audible come from our own listening, not a controlled test.
  • No users or engineers have been studied; the workflow around the model is untested.
  • Compressor ratio, attack and release remain near the guessing level.
  • Delay feedback and reverb width are also near guessing. In our own listening, not a controlled test, reverb width is close to inaudible on our material.

How MRSTFT is computed

  1. The model sees the dry stems and the processed stems once and predicts a chain: 44 switches and 112 knob values.
  2. The dry stems are rendered once through that chain with the same effect strip.
  3. For each stem, left and right, the render is compared with the processed version using a multi-resolution STFT distance. Both are turned into magnitude spectrograms at three window sizes (FFT 512, 1024 and 2048 with hop sizes 128, 256 and 512, Hann windows the length of the FFT, and an epsilon of 1e-5 inside the log), and spectral convergence plus the L1 distance of log magnitudes is averaged over the three. We use several window sizes because any single STFT trades time detail for frequency detail, so short windows catch transients (e.g., a clipped drum hit) and long ones catch tone (e.g., an EQ boost), and a distance at one size would be partly blind to one of them. This is the distance StemFX uses, which keeps our numbers comparable with theirs.
  4. The 8 channels (4 stems, left and right) are averaged, with stems that are silent in a clip zeroed so they do not count, which gives MRSTFT for one query; the reported number is the mean over the 1,000 queries.

A score of 0 means the render is identical to the processed version, and re-rendering the true chain scores close to 0, while handing back the dry audio unchanged scores 1.17. MRSTFT is unitless and computed per stem rather than on a summed mix, so an error on one stem cannot hide under the others in the sum. It measures a spectral match and is not a listening score.

Knob skill is 1 minus the model's knob error divided by the error of always guessing the typical value, on stems where the effect is on. The scale each knob is scored on is in the knob table.

Data: FMA stems split with SCNet, plus MoisesDB studio stems. Public datasets and open-source effects only. No UA plugins, models or data.
